WebP vs JPEG Generation Loss Compared

Generational loss occurs when a lossy image format undergoes repeated cycles of decoding, editing, and re-encoding, resulting in cumulative visual artifacts and data destruction. This comparison explores how modern lossy WebP handles repeated re-compression compared to standard JPEG, examining the underlying mathematical causes, the distinct visual patterns of their degradation, and which format better preserves image integrity across multiple generations.

How Lossy Compression Causes Generational Decay

Both lossy WebP and JPEG discard data that the human visual system is less likely to notice. When an image is re-encoded, the compression algorithm treats the previously introduced artifacts as original image data. It compresses the existing errors, adds new approximations, and quantizes the color and frequency values again. Over successive generations, this compounds into severe visual distortion.

JPEG Generational Degradation

JPEG relies on the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) applied to fixed \(8 \times 8\) pixel blocks, followed by quantization and chroma subsampling.

The Artifact Profile

  • Blockiness: As high-frequency data is stripped away across generations, the boundaries of the \(8 \times 8\) pixel grids become increasingly prominent, producing a visible checkerboard pattern.
  • Mosquito Noise and Ringing: High-contrast edges develop high-frequency halos and buzzing artifacts due to repeated truncation of high-frequency DCT coefficients.
  • Color Drift: Successive passes of chroma subsampling (typically 4:2:0) cause colors to bleed, desaturate, or shift in hue.

Re-encoding Behavior

If a JPEG is repeatedly re-saved at the exact same quality setting without modifying its pixel dimensions or aligning shifts, the rate of degradation can eventually plateau. However, if the image is cropped, translated off the \(8 \times 8\) grid, or saved at fluctuating quality levels, degradation accelerates catastrophically, creating misaligned overlapping block grids.

WebP Generational Degradation

Lossy WebP is derived from the VP8 video codec keyframe architecture. Instead of treating every block independently like standard JPEG, WebP relies heavily on spatial intra-prediction and dynamic sub-blocks (\(4 \times 4\) or \(16 \times 16\)), combined with an aggressive in-loop deblocking filter.

The Artifact Profile

  • Smearing and Posterization: Because WebP predicts pixel blocks from neighboring pixels, generational errors propagate spatially. Instead of forming rigid grids, fine textures gradually blur and merge into flat, waxy surfaces.
  • Watercolor Effect: Repeated application of WebP's built-in deblocking filter smooths away high-frequency noise, yielding an illustrative, painterly appearance rather than a noisy one.
  • Edge Warping: Sharp transitions tend to stay sharper longer than in JPEG without ringing, but the surrounding fine details bleed into the background.

Re-encoding Behavior

Because WebP dynamically predicts blocks based on adjacent context, generational loss spreads unevenly across the image. Repeated saves at identical settings do not plateau as reliably as JPEG because the prediction vectors continuously recalculate based on the smoothed, filtered output of the previous generation.

Which Format Holds Up Better?

At moderate to high quality settings (Q80+), JPEG tends to maintain structural consistency and high-frequency textures slightly better over dozens of identical, unshifted re-saves, despite its obvious block artifacts. WebP prioritizes eliminating visible noise, meaning it strips fine details faster with each generation, turning complex surfaces like skin, fabric, and foliage into flat color fields.

At low quality settings (below Q50), WebP survives generational cycles with fewer jarring structural breakdowns than JPEG. While a JPEG quickly breaks down into an unreadable grid of pixel blocks, WebP retains the primary silhouettes and structural shapes of the original image, albeit heavily smoothed.
