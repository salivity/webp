Why Lossy WebP Fails for Digital Elevation Models

Digital Elevation Models (DEMs) serve as foundational datasets for spatial analysis, hydrology, and engineering, requiring strict mathematical accuracy rather than mere visual appeal. While lossy WebP is a highly efficient format for standard web imagery, applying it to DEMs degrades elevation values, creates geometric artifacts, and eliminates the data depth required for scientific calculations. This article explains why lossy WebP is technically fundamentally unsuited for storing elevation rasters and conducting geospatial analysis.

Data Destruction Through Perceptual Compression

Lossy WebP relies on the VP8 video codec, which utilizes predictive coding and discrete cosine transforms designed specifically around the Human Visual System (HVS). This algorithm deliberately discards high-frequency information and smooths subtle gradients under the assumption that the human eye cannot perceive the difference. In a DEM, however, pixel values do not represent color; they represent precise z-coordinate measurements (meters or feet above a datum). Applying perceptual lossy compression alters actual physical measurements, replacing true ground elevations with approximations that invalidate any downstream quantitative measurement.

Inadequate Bit Depth and Dynamic Range

Real-world terrain data requires high vertical precision, typically delivered via 16-bit integer or 32-bit floating-point rasters. Lossy WebP is inherently restricted to an 8-bit color space (YUV420). Compressing elevation into an 8-bit space restricts the entire vertical range to only 256 discrete values. Even across moderate topography, this results in severe vertical quantization, turning smooth hillsides into a series of unnatural, flat horizontal steps—a phenomenon known as banding or terracing.

Ruined Derivative Analyses (Slope, Aspect, and Hydrology)

Geospatial algorithms depend heavily on local neighborhood calculations. When calculating first and second derivatives from a DEM—such as slope, aspect, curvature, and surface roughness—the mathematical differences between adjacent cells (\(dz/dx\) and \(dz/dy\)) are paramount. Lossy WebP introduces macroblock boundary artifacts (typically in \(4\times4\) or \(16\times16\) pixel blocks). In derivative models, these block edges produce artificial linear ridges, false cliffs, and synthetic steps that render hydrologic flow routing, watershed delineation, and landslide susceptibility models entirely useless.

Distortion of Void and NoData Masking

Scientific rasters rely on specific reserved values (such as -9999 or NaN) to represent missing data, water bodies, or boundaries outside the area of interest. Lossy compression blends boundaries between valid data cells and NoData cells. This edge-blending corrupts boundary values into false elevations, generating invalid extreme heights or phantom depressions along the edges of the dataset.

Absence of Native Spatial Metadata

Formats purpose-built for GIS, such as Cloud Optimized GeoTIFF (COG), embed critical coordinate reference systems (CRS), bounding box coordinates, and resolution parameters directly within the file structure. Standard WebP lacks native support for geospatial metadata tags. Without custom wrappers or fragile sidecar files, GIS engines cannot georeference the data accurately in coordinate space.

Suitable Alternatives

When web-friendly compression of elevation data is required, lossless formats that support higher bit depths should be used instead:

  • Cloud Optimized GeoTIFF (COG) with LZW, Deflate, or ZSTD compression preserves exact floating-point precision while remaining efficient for streaming.
  • Mapbox Terrain-RGB or Terrarium formats encode 24-bit elevation values into the Red, Green, and Blue channels of standard PNGs, delivering lossless precision over standard image protocols.
  • Quantized Mesh formats provide optimized, triangulated surface geometry ideal for 3D terrain rendering without corrupting the underlying spatial integrity.
