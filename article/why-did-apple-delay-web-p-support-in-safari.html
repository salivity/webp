Why Did Apple Delay WebP Support in Safari?

Apple delayed native WebP support in Safari for a decade, finally introducing it in 2020 with iOS 14 and macOS Big Sur. This delay was primarily driven by strategic resistance to Google-dominated web standards, the absence of dedicated hardware decoding which threatened battery life, concerns regarding image quality and color management in early versions of the format, and Apple’s heavy investment in alternative image compression technologies like HEIF.

Strategic Resistance to Google Standards

Google released WebP in 2010 to reduce image sizes across the web, quickly integrating it into Chrome and Android. Apple has historically resisted adopting web formats championed primarily by a single competitor, particularly when those formats are pushed onto the open web without broad consensus through traditional standardization bodies like the W3C or ISO. Apple favored maintaining engine autonomy in WebKit rather than following Google's Blink engine specifications, fearing that rapid adoption of proprietary-leaning technologies would accelerate Google's web monoculture.

Battery Life and Hardware Acceleration

Hardware efficiency and battery optimization are core pillars of Apple's device strategy. Traditional formats like JPEG and H.264 video benefit from dedicated silicon decoders built into Apple’s A-series and M-series chips. Because older Apple chips lacked hardware-level decoding for WebP, decoding the format required software rendering through the CPU. This raised CPU usage, generated more heat, and drained battery life on mobile devices like iPhones and iPads. Apple routinely avoids native web feature rollouts until the underlying performance impact meets its mobile efficiency standards.

Investment in HEIC and HEIF

While Google advanced WebP, Apple invested heavily in the High Efficiency Image File Format (HEIF/HEIC), developed by the Moving Picture Experts Group (MPEG). Introduced across iOS and macOS in 2017, HEIF offered superior compression efficiency, 16-bit color capability, and support for multi-image sequences (such as Live Photos) compared to WebP’s original design. Because Apple had already committed its hardware encoders and decoders to HEIF, allocating engineering resources to integrate a rival standard was a lower priority.

Quality and Color Limitations of Early WebP

Initial iterations of WebP faced technical criticism from developers and imaging experts. Early versions exhibited noticeable loss of fine detail and texture, often producing blurring artifacts in complex photographic areas compared to properly optimized JPEG files. Furthermore, early WebP had limited support for advanced color profiles (such as Apple's preferred Display P3 wide color gamut) and lacked native high-dynamic-range (HDR) capabilities. Apple held off until the format matured and addressed these functional shortcomings.

The Shift to Adoption

By 2020, WebP had evolved into a stable, versatile format with widespread cross-platform adoption. Major Content Delivery Networks (CDNs), web servers, and rival browsers universally supported it, and a significant portion of modern web traffic relied on it for reduced payload sizes. As the performance gap narrowed and omitting support began causing broken web experiences for Safari users, Apple finally implemented native WebP decoding across its ecosystem.
