Are WebP Files Already Entropy Compressed?

This article examines whether WebP bitstreams are fully entropy-compressed before HTTP transmission and why applying transport-layer compression like Gzip or Brotli is redundant. It explains the internal compression mechanics of both lossy and lossless WebP formats, how their respective entropy coders operate, and the recommended server configurations for delivering WebP assets over the web.

WebP bitstreams are fully entropy-compressed at the time of file creation, long before reaching the network layer. Both the lossy and lossless variants of the format incorporate advanced entropy-coding stages designed to eliminate statistical redundancy, leaving the resulting bitstream at or near its theoretical entropy limit.

For lossy WebP, which is based on the VP8 video codec container, compression begins with macroblock prediction followed by discrete cosine transforms (DCT) and coefficient quantization. The resulting syntax elements, motion vectors, and quantized transform coefficients are then processed through a Boolean arithmetic coder. Arithmetic coding is a form of entropy encoding that maps variable-probability symbols to fractional bits, packing the data stream far more efficiently than standard Huffman coding and eliminating practically all remaining statistical redundancy.

For lossless WebP (VP8L), the pipeline uses several image-specific modeling transforms, including spatial prediction, color space transformation, and a local color cache. Once these transforms reduce pixel correlation, the residual data is passed through an LZ77 dictionary coder to remove repeated sequences. Finally, the remaining literals and match lengths are compressed using canonical Huffman coding with adaptive, sub-image-specific codebooks. This multi-tiered process produces a dense, fully compressed binary stream.

Because both variations of WebP emit high-entropy binary streams, applying HTTP-level compression algorithms—such as Gzip (DEFLATE), Brotli, or Zstandard—provides virtually no benefit. Transport-layer compression relies on detecting recurring patterns and statistical imbalances. When applied to an already entropy-coded WebP stream, these algorithms cannot identify repeatable sequences. Attempting to re-compress the data typically produces one of two outcomes: the file size remains effectively unchanged, or it expands slightly due to the overhead of HTTP compression framing and metadata.

Furthermore, compressing WebP files dynamically on an HTTP server wastes CPU cycles on both compression at the origin and decompression on the client browser. Web servers and content delivery networks (CDNs) should explicitly exclude .webp files from Gzip and Brotli compression configurations, serving them directly with their native Content-Type: image/webp header.
