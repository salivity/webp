WebP vs HEVC Licensing: Open vs Royalty-Encumbered

This article compares the licensing frameworks of Google's WebP and the High Efficiency Video Coding (HEVC/H.265) standard, highlighting how their legal structures impact developer adoption, browser integration, and commercial deployment. While WebP relies on an open-source, royalty-free distribution model designed for universal web accessibility, HEVC is governed by a fragmented, royalty-encumbered patent landscape that imposes financial and administrative burdens on hardware and software creators.

WebP: The Royalty-Free, Open-Source Model

Google introduced WebP to accelerate the web by providing an efficient, modern image format. Derived from the VP8 video codec, WebP is distributed under a permissive three-clause BSD license accompanied by an explicit, irrevocable patent grant from Google.

Under this model:

  • No Licensing Fees: Software developers, browser vendors, content delivery networks (CDNs), and end users can encode, decode, and distribute WebP images without paying royalties.
  • No Administrative Burden: Organizations do not need to negotiate terms, track device distribution counts, or sign agreements with licensing administrators.
  • Patent Protection: Google's grant protects users from patent infringement claims by Google regarding the underlying technology, ensuring legal predictability for open-source and proprietary software alike.

HEVC: The Royalty-Encumbered Model

HEVC (H.265), along with its image adaptation BPG (Better Portable Graphics) and HEIC container implementations, was developed jointly by the ISO/IEC Moving Picture Experts Group (MPEG) and ITU-T. Unlike WebP, HEVC is proprietary technology covered by thousands of essential patents owned by dozens of commercial entities.

The HEVC licensing landscape is characterized by:

  • Multiple Patent Pools: Patents are divided across separate licensing administrators—including MPEG LA, Access Advance (formerly HEVC Advance), and Velos Media—in addition to individual patent holders who do not belong to any pool.
  • Direct Financial Costs: Commercial implementers must pay per-unit royalties for hardware devices and, in some contexts, software implementations and content distribution platforms.
  • Fragmented Terms: Because there is no single licensing body, companies must navigate distinct fee structures, annual royalty caps, and reporting requirements across multiple entities.

Key Differences and Impact on Adoption

Feature WebP HEVC
Licensing Cost 100% Free Per-unit and platform royalties
License Type Permissive Open Source (BSD) Proprietary Commercial Patent Pools
Legal Administration None required Complex contracts, auditing, reporting
Software Support Native in all major browsers Limited native software support due to licensing fees

The primary divergence between the two models lies in software-only distribution. Because HEVC licensing often requires payments for software decoders distributed to consumers, open-source projects like Mozilla Firefox and Chromium historically excluded native software support to avoid patent liabilities. As a result, HEVC playback on the web largely depends on whether the underlying operating system and hardware have already paid for the license.

Conversely, WebP's zero-cost license removed the financial barriers that prevent open-source software and smaller developers from adopting next-generation compression. This legal clarity is the primary reason WebP achieved universal support across all modern web browsers, whereas HEVC and related formats remain largely confined to licensed hardware ecosystems, operating systems, and specialized commercial media pipelines.
