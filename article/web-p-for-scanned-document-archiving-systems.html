WebP for Scanned Document Archiving Systems

This article evaluates whether Google's WebP image format can serve as a standardized format for scanned document archival systems. While WebP provides superior compression efficiency, metadata support, and broad web compatibility, long-term archival environments demand strict adherence to longevity, multi-page document handling, independent standardization, and text layer integration. Below is an analysis of WebP's technical capabilities, its limitations in preservation workflows, and how it compares to established standards like TIFF and PDF/A.

Technical Advantages of WebP

WebP provides several technical benefits that make it attractive for digital storage:

  • High Compression Efficiency: WebP supports both lossy and lossless compression. Its lossless mode compresses images 26% better than PNG on average, while its lossy mode achieves 25% to 34% smaller file sizes than comparable JPEG images. For document repositories storing millions of scanned pages, this results in significant storage and bandwidth cost reductions.
  • Metadata Support: The format supports embedded metadata via XMP and EXIF standards, allowing document indexing details, provenance, and capture device information to remain bound to the file.
  • Color Profile and Transparency Support: WebP accommodates 24-bit color, alpha channel transparency, and embedded ICC profiles, ensuring that color reproduction remains consistent across different scanning hardware.

Archival Requirements vs. WebP Capabilities

Document archiving differs from standard image storage because preservation systems prioritize readability over decades, legal admissibility, and information completeness.

1. Multi-Page Support

Most scanned business, legal, and historical records consist of multi-page documents. WebP is primarily a single-image format. While it supports animation frames (which some systems repurpose for multi-page bundling), there is no standardized mechanism within the format for document pagination, page-level metadata indexing, or non-temporal page navigation. Formats like TIFF and PDF natively support multi-page structures.

2. Searchable Text (OCR) Integration

Archival standards such as PDF/A allow dual-layer storage where the scanned raster image is paired with a hidden, searchable text layer generated by Optical Character Recognition (OCR). WebP is strictly a raster graphics container. It cannot natively store structured, searchable text layers or font vectors, meaning an archive using WebP must maintain OCR data in external sidecar files (such as ALTO XML or JSON), increasing the risk of file detachment over time.

3. Formal Standardization and Governance

Preservation standards rely heavily on stability managed by independent bodies. PDF/A (ISO 19005) and TIFF (de facto industry standard governed by Adobe) have long histories of strict backwards compatibility. WebP is an open standard developed and maintained primarily by Google. Because it lacks an independent ISO certification for preservation, institutional archives (such as national libraries and legal repositories) often disqualify it under their digital preservation mandates.

4. Ecosystem Compatibility

Document management systems (EDMS), high-volume production scanners, and legal discovery tools are engineered around PDF and TIFF pipelines. Although modern web browsers and image viewers support WebP, specialized enterprise archival software often requires third-party plugins or conversion layers to ingest and render WebP files.

Feasibility Verdict

WebP cannot currently replace PDF/A or TIFF as a standardized master format for scanned document archival systems. The lack of native multi-page structuring, the inability to embed searchable OCR text layers directly, and the absence of an ISO preservation standard make it unsuitable for long-term legal and historical preservation.

However, WebP is highly effective as a secondary "access" or "derivative" format. Archival systems can retain masters in uncompressed TIFF or PDF/A-1b, while dynamically generating WebP files to deliver fast, low-bandwidth document previews to end users over the web.
