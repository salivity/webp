Is WebP Royalty-Free and Open for Public Use?

This article examines the legal and licensing status of the WebP image format, specifically whether it is completely free from patent royalties and available for unrestricted public use. It covers Google’s licensing terms, the patent grant accompanying the software, historical patent pool challenges involving the underlying VP8 technology, and what this means for developers and businesses deploying WebP today.

WebP is an open-source image format developed by Google that provides lossy and lossless compression for web images. From a practical and commercial standpoint, WebP is free to use without paying patent royalties. Google released the source code under a permissive, 3-clause BSD license and paired it with an explicit, worldwide, royalty-free patent license. This patent grant allows anyone to use, copy, modify, and distribute WebP implementations without paying licensing fees to Google.

Because WebP’s lossy compression algorithm is based on the VP8 video codec, it inherited some of the patent scrutiny previously directed at VP8. Around 2011, the MPEG LA organization attempted to form a patent pool claiming that VP8 infringed upon patents owned by various telecommunications and media companies. However, in March 2013, Google reached a definitive agreement with MPEG LA and 11 patent holders. Google acquired licenses for those patents and secured the right to sublicense them to any VP8 or WebP user under royalty-free terms, effectively resolving the threat of third-party patent infringement from that group.

While WebP is royalty-free, describing it as having "unrestricted" use requires a distinction between copyright licensing, patent rights, and conditions of use:

  1. License Conditions: The underlying code uses the BSD license, which requires retaining copyright notices and disclaimer texts when distributing the source or binary code.
  2. Patent Retaliation Clause: Google’s patent grant contains a defensive termination clause. If an entity initiates patent litigation against Google or other parties alleging that the WebP software infringes their patents, their royalty-free license to Google's WebP patents is revoked.
  3. General Software Patent Risk: As with almost any modern digital technology (including older standards like JPEG), it is impossible to guarantee that no unknown third party will ever assert an unrelated patent claim. However, no active, successful royalty demands exist against WebP.

For websites, software developers, and commercial enterprises, WebP can be considered fully royalty-free and open for public and commercial adoption. You do not need to purchase a license, pay royalties, or seek special authorization to encode, decode, or display WebP images.
