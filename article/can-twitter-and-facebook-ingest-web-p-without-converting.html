Can Twitter and Facebook Ingest WebP Without Converting?

While major social media platforms like Twitter (X) and Facebook allow users to upload WebP images, they do not ingest or store them without converting. Instead, both platforms automatically transcode WebP uploads into traditional formats—primarily JPEG or PNG—during their backend image processing pipelines to ensure universal compatibility and maintain optimized delivery across all devices.

How Twitter (X) Handles WebP

Twitter allows you to select and upload .webp files through its web interface and mobile applications. However, upon ingestion, Twitter’s media processing pipeline immediately converts static WebP images into standard JPEGs.

If you upload an animated WebP file, Twitter does not preserve the format; it either flattens the file into a static image or converts the animation into an MP4 video or GIF loop, depending on how the upload is handled. When visitors view or download your image from Twitter, the delivered asset will have a .jpg extension rather than the original .webp.

How Facebook Handles WebP

Facebook accepts WebP uploads but follows a similar conversion protocol. Once an image is uploaded, Facebook’s pipeline strips extraneous metadata, resizes the file to generate multiple display resolutions, and converts the image into JPEG or PNG format.

Although Facebook uses modern compression algorithms internally to serve lightweight images across its content delivery network (CDN), it does not retain the original WebP file you uploaded for public delivery.

Why Social Networks Convert WebP Files

Social media platforms transcode uploaded images rather than serving the raw files for several technical reasons:

  • Universal Compatibility: Despite widespread modern adoption of WebP, platforms must ensure that images render reliably on legacy web browsers, older mobile operating systems, and third-party API integrations.
  • Standardized Compression: Platforms enforce their own proprietary compression algorithms to control bandwidth costs and maintain consistent visual quality across billions of feeds.
  • Privacy and Security: Re-encoding images strips out potentially sensitive EXIF data, such as GPS coordinates and camera serial numbers, while mitigating vulnerabilities linked to corrupted image headers.

In summary, while Twitter and Facebook accept WebP files at the point of upload, neither platform retains or serves the original WebP format without converting it first.
