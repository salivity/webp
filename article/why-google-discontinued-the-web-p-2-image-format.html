Why Google Discontinued the WebP 2 Image Format

Google originally developed WebP 2 (wp2) as the successor to the widely adopted WebP format, aiming to provide superior compression efficiency and advanced features for modern web graphics. However, the project was officially discontinued as a prospective image format release and transitioned into an internal research testbed. This decision was primarily driven by the rapid rise of competing formats like AVIF and JPEG XL, diminishing returns in compression gains, and the practical challenges of introducing another image standard to an already fragmented web ecosystem.

The Rise of Competing Next-Gen Standards

During WebP 2's development cycle, rival next-generation formats matured much faster than anticipated. AVIF (AV1 Image File Format), backed by the Alliance for Open Media—which includes Google, Apple, Mozilla, and Microsoft—quickly gained widespread industry support and native browser integration. Because AVIF leveraged the existing AV1 video codec hardware acceleration, it achieved rapid cross-platform adoption. At the same time, JPEG XL emerged as a versatile alternative offering unique benefits, such as lossless transcoding of legacy JPEG files. With these formats actively gaining traction, the necessity for another standalone format diminished.

Marginal Compression Advantages

WebP 2 aimed to improve compression density by roughly 20% compared to original WebP, matching the efficiency of AVIF. However, benchmarks revealed that WebP 2 did not offer a significant enough performance leap over AVIF or JPEG XL to justify its adoption. For a new image format to succeed globally, it must offer generational improvements that clearly surpass existing technologies; WebP 2’s performance parity with existing options meant there was little technical incentive for the broader industry to switch.

Preventing Ecosystem Fragmentation

Introducing a new image format to the web requires immense coordination across web browsers, operating systems, content delivery networks (CDNs), and image editing software. Maintaining multiple overlapping image decoders introduces security vulnerabilities, bloats browser binaries, and complicates workflows for web developers who must serve multiple fallbacks. Google recognized that releasing WebP 2 would fragment the ecosystem without solving problems that AVIF and original WebP were not already addressing.

Transition to a Compression Research Testbed

Rather than discarding the work, the development team repurposed the WebP 2 repository as an experimental testbed. Google updated the project's documentation to clarify that WebP 2 would not see an official format release. Instead, the algorithms, transforms, and heuristics engineered during the project now serve as research tools to inform future image and video compression technologies.
