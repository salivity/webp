Why JPEG XR Lost to Google WebP

In the late 2000s and early 2010s, both Microsoft and Google attempted to replace the aging standard JPEG format with modern alternatives: Microsoft introduced JPEG XR, while Google developed WebP. Despite JPEG XR offering impressive compression efficiency and high-dynamic-range support, it failed to secure commercial momentum and was ultimately eclipsed by WebP. This outcome was primarily driven by Google’s control over the modern web ecosystem, open-source licensing advantages, superior platform reach, and WebP’s direct alignment with web performance needs.

Open-Source Accessibility vs. Proprietary Perception

Google released WebP under a permissive BSD-style free software license, providing ready-to-use, open-source reference implementations (libwebp) for developers and webmasters. This encouraged immediate experimentation and integration into third-party tools, content delivery networks (CDNs), and graphics software. Conversely, even though Microsoft standardized JPEG XR through the ITU-T and ISO/IEC, the format originated as Windows Media Photo (HD Photo). It carried the lingering perception of being tied to Microsoft’s proprietary ecosystem and patent landscape, which generated hesitation within open-source and cross-platform communities.

Browser Dominance and Cross-Platform Reach

Format adoption on the web relies entirely on client support. Google leveraged the meteoric rise of the Chrome browser and the Android mobile operating system to deploy native WebP decoding to billions of endpoints rapidly. Microsoft, on the other hand, confined JPEG XR support largely to Internet Explorer, early versions of Microsoft Edge, and the Windows desktop environment. Because JPEG XR was never natively adopted by Apple (Safari/iOS) or Mozilla (Firefox), and lacked an aggressive push onto mobile operating systems, web developers could not rely on it as a universal format without complex fallbacks.

Purpose-Built for Web Performance

JPEG XR was engineered with professional digital photography, high-end printing, and high-bit-depth color spaces (such as HDR) in mind. While technically capable, its primary strengths did not solve the most pressing problems of the web. In contrast, WebP was explicitly built to accelerate web page loading speeds. Derived from the VP8 video codec, WebP combined lossy compression with features that web designers critically needed: 24-bit color with 8-bit alpha transparency (providing a lightweight alternative to heavy PNG files) and animation support to challenge the inefficient GIF format.

First-Party Ecosystem Leverage

Google actively validated WebP's viability by deploying it across its own massive web properties, including the Google Play Store, YouTube image thumbnails, and the Picasa/Google Photos ecosystem. By proving substantial bandwidth and cost savings at enterprise scale, Google compelled major CDNs like Cloudflare to automate WebP conversion for websites worldwide. Microsoft did not apply equivalent pressure across its consumer-facing web services to force industry adoption.

The Final Transition

The commercial contest effectively ended when Microsoft abandoned its proprietary EdgeHTML engine in 2019 to rebuild Microsoft Edge on Google's open-source Chromium codebase. With that architectural transition, Microsoft adopted native support for WebP while effectively deprecating JPEG XR as a web standard.
