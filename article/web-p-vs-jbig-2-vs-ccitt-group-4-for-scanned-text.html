WebP vs JBIG2 vs CCITT Group 4 for Scanned Text

When archiving scanned black-and-white (bi-tonal) text documents, choosing the right compression algorithm directly impacts file size, text legibility, and system performance. While WebP has emerged as a premier modern standard for full-color web imagery, it is generally outperformed by specialized document formats like JBIG2 and CCITT Group 4 when handling pure 1-bit black-and-white data. This article compares how WebP, JBIG2, and CCITT Group 4 process bi-tonal scanned text, highlighting their technical differences, compression efficiency, and practical use cases.

Understanding Bi-tonal Scans

Scanned black-and-white documents are strictly bi-tonal, meaning every pixel is represented by a single bit: 0 for white and 1 for black. Unlike photographic images, these documents contain sharp edges, vast empty white backgrounds, and recurring geometric patterns in the form of font characters. Efficient document compression relies heavily on exploiting these unique characteristics.

CCITT Group 4: The Legacy Standard

CCITT Group 4 (also known as ITU-T T.6 or Fax 4) is a lossless compression algorithm developed specifically for 1-bit bi-tonal document transmission and archiving.

  • How it works: Group 4 utilizes a two-dimensional line-by-line coding technique. It compresses data by tracking the transitions between black and white pixels on a given line relative to the transitions on the line immediately preceding it.
  • Strengths: It is exceptionally fast to encode and decode, requires minimal computational overhead, and is universally supported across virtually all PDF readers, TIFF viewers, and enterprise document management systems.
  • Limitations: It processes lines sequentially and does not recognize recurring shapes across the entire page, resulting in larger file sizes compared to more modern document-specific codecs.

JBIG2: The Document Archiving Champion

JBIG2 (ITU-T T.88) was engineered specifically to supersede CCITT Group 4 for bi-tonal images, offering substantially higher compression ratios.

  • How it works: JBIG2 segments an image into distinct regions: text, halftone patterns, and general graphics. For text, it scans the page to build a "dictionary" of unique character shapes (glyphs). When a character repeats—such as the letter "e"—JBIG2 records only the coordinate position of the matching dictionary entry rather than re-encoding the pixels.
  • Strengths: JBIG2 achieves file sizes that are typically 2 to 5 times smaller than CCITT Group 4 while retaining pristine visual sharpness in lossless mode. It is a standard component of the PDF format.
  • Limitations: Encoding requires significantly more processing power due to the pattern-matching algorithms. Improper configuration of lossy JBIG2 mode can lead to character substitution errors, making strict lossless mode essential for sensitive legal or financial archives.

WebP: The General-Purpose Web Format

WebP was created by Google to optimize 24-bit photographic images and 32-bit transparent graphics for the web, based on VP8 video keyframe encoding.

  • How it works: WebP does not natively operate on 1-bit binary arrays. It treats images as continuous-tone data (usually 8-bit grayscale or 24-bit RGB), utilizing spatial prediction, color transform modeling, and LZ77-based entropy coding.
  • Lossy WebP: Applying lossy WebP to scanned text creates noticeable ringing artifacts and smudging around character edges, which degrades legibility and interferes with Optical Character Recognition (OCR) engines.
  • Lossless WebP: Lossless WebP preserves sharp edges, but because it is not designed to create font glyph dictionaries or native 1-bit run-length trees, it cannot match the structural efficiency of dedicated bi-tonal codecs. A bi-tonal image saved as a lossless WebP is typically larger than the same image compressed with JBIG2, and often larger than or comparable to CCITT Group 4.

Conclusion

For storing pure black-and-white scanned text, JBIG2 remains the superior format, delivering the smallest possible file sizes through its character-dictionary compression without sacrificing legibility. CCITT Group 4 remains the most dependable choice when processing speed and legacy compatibility across older TIFF and PDF systems are required.

WebP is not recommended for pure bi-tonal text storage. Because it lacks native 1-bit encoding and symbol-matching capabilities, it yields larger file sizes and offers no technical advantages over JBIG2 or CCITT Group 4 in document archiving pipelines. WebP is best reserved for documents that contain full-color photographs, continuous-tone illustrations, or color backgrounds.
