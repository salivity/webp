Why WebP Hardware Encoding Is Less Common Than JPEG

While hardware-accelerated JPEG encoding is ubiquitous across virtually every modern smartphone, camera, and processor, dedicated hardware encoding support for WebP remains exceptionally rare. This article explores the primary reasons for this disparity, examining JPEG's entrenched market legacy, the architectural complexity of WebP, the economics of semiconductor design, and the industry’s strategic decision to bypass WebP in favor of next-generation formats like AVIF.

Three Decades of Entrenched Legacy

JPEG was standardized in 1992, decades before mobile computing and modern internet architectures matured. As digital cameras and camera-equipped smartphones emerged, hardware manufacturers designed dedicated Image Signal Processors (ISPs) directly around the JPEG standard. Because billions of devices required instantaneous photo capture with minimal battery drain, JPEG encoding circuits became a permanent, non-negotiable component of modern silicon architectures. In contrast, Google introduced WebP in 2010 primarily as a web delivery format rather than a capture format, meaning the initial demand for real-time, on-chip capture support was virtually nonexistent.

Computational Complexity and Codec Heritage

Lossy WebP is built upon the keyframe (intra-frame) compression architecture of the VP8 video codec. Unlike JPEG’s relatively straightforward Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) pipeline, WebP relies on complex predictive coding techniques to estimate block content based on adjacent pixels. Designing dedicated fixed-function silicon blocks to handle VP8-based still-image encoding requires significantly more logic gates and memory bandwidth. Because general-purpose CPUs became powerful enough to encode WebP files in software within web servers and content delivery networks, hardware vendors saw little reason to design fixed-function silicon for it.

Silicon Area and Economics

In semiconductor manufacturing, silicon area equates directly to cost, thermal output, and power consumption. System-on-Chip (SoC) designers like Qualcomm, Apple, and Intel must justify every square millimeter of silicon added to a chip. Hardware acceleration is reserved for operations that are either too slow or too power-hungry to execute in software—such as real-time 4K video recording (H.264, HEVC) or continuous camera burst captures (JPEG). Since WebP encoding is typically performed on backend servers rather than edge capture devices, consumer chipmakers had no economic incentive to waste silicon area on dedicated WebP encoding engines.

Delayed Industry Adoption

WebP suffered from a protracted adoption cycle. Major platforms resisted supporting the format for nearly a decade; notably, Apple did not add comprehensive native support for WebP to Safari and iOS until 2020. By the time WebP attained universal browser and operating system compatibility, the tech industry was already developing newer, more capable image formats based on modern video standards.

The Shift Toward Next-Generation Standards

Instead of implementing hardware support for an aging VP8-derived format, hardware manufacturers focused their resources on forward-looking standards. Technologies such as AVIF (derived from the AV1 video codec) offer superior compression efficiency compared to WebP. Because major hardware and software vendors were already investing billions into adding AV1 video decoders and encoders into their latest chipsets, the AVIF format naturally inherited hardware acceleration paths. Consequently, WebP was largely bypassed at the hardware level, remaining primarily a CPU-bound format optimized for delivery across the web.
