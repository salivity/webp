Lossy WebP Repeated Compression Artifacts Explained

When a lossy WebP image is repeatedly saved, decoded, and re-compressed, it undergoes generation loss. Because lossy WebP is derived from the VP8 video codec, its degradation patterns differ noticeably from traditional formats like JPEG. Over successive compression cycles, mathematical rounding errors and aggressive quantization compound, transforming sharp imagery into an over-smoothed, distorted version of the original.

Progressive Blurring and the "Plastic" Effect

The most prominent artifact of generational WebP compression is extreme smoothing. Lossy WebP relies heavily on intra-frame prediction and an aggressive built-in loop filter designed to prevent visible block seams. With each re-compression, high-frequency spatial data is discarded. Fine textures—such as skin pores, fabric weaves, grain, or foliage—are rapidly smoothed away, leaving surfaces with a waxy, plastic-like appearance.

Macroblocking and Prediction Drift

WebP processes images using 16x16 macroblocks divided into smaller sub-blocks (4x4 luminance and 8x8 chrominance) to predict pixel values from neighboring blocks. In repeated re-encoding passes:

  • The encoder may choose different prediction modes than the original pass due to slightly modified boundary pixels.
  • Faint grid patterns and misaligned square blocks emerge across what should be continuous regions.
  • While the deblocking filter tries to blur these edges, heavy re-compression eventually overwhelms the filter, causing block boundaries to become plainly visible.

Ringing and Halo Artifacts

High-contrast edges, such as dark text on a light background or sharp silhouettes, suffer from ringing artifacts (often called mosquito noise). This occurs because lossy WebP uses discrete cosine transform (DCT) and Walsh-Hadamard transforms. Quantizing transform coefficients discards higher frequencies, causing high-contrast transitions to generate rippling echoes or blurry halos along sharp outlines.

Chroma Degradation and Color Banding

Standard lossy WebP converts RGB images to the YUV color space and applies 4:2:0 chroma subsampling, cutting color resolution in half both horizontally and vertically. Across repeated compressions:

  • Color Bleeding: Saturated colors spill over into neighboring neutral zones.
  • Posterization (Banding): Smooth gradients (such as sunsets or clear skies) break down into stark, stepped bands of flat color as intermediate color values are quantized out of existence.
  • Loss of Color Depth: Subtle chroma variations disappear entirely, leading to flat, dull tones.

Luminance Shifts and Rounding Drift

Every compression cycle requires converting from RGB to YUV for encoding, and then from YUV back to RGB for display or decoding. Because these color-space transformations involve floating-point approximations truncated to integer values, microscopic rounding errors accumulate. Over dozens of generations, the image may exhibit noticeable shifts in overall brightness, contrast collapse, or an unintended color tint across neutral gray areas.
