Why Animated WebP Struggled to Replace GIF

Despite offering vastly superior compression, 24-bit color, and alpha transparency, animated WebP faced a sluggish, multi-year path to mainstream adoption after its introduction by Google. While the Graphics Interchange Format (GIF) was technically obsolete—limited to an inefficient 256-color palette and massive file sizes—it remained deeply entrenched across the web. Animated WebP struggled to unseat GIF immediately due to fragmented browser support, an absence of creation and editing tools, the rapid emergence of looped video alternatives, and the massive cultural inertia behind the GIF standard.

Delayed Browser and Ecosystem Support

A web format requires near-universal browser support before developers can safely implement it in production. While Google Chrome added support for animated WebP in 2013, other major browsers lagged significantly:

  • Apple's Safari Resistance: Apple did not add native WebP support to Safari and iOS until late 2020 (Safari 14). Because a massive share of mobile web traffic relies on WebKit and iOS, developers could not switch exclusively to WebP without building complex fallbacks.
  • Mozilla Firefox: Firefox did not introduce default WebP support until early 2019 (Firefox 65), years after the format debuted.
  • Legacy Browsers: Internet Explorer never natively supported WebP, forcing enterprise environments and backwards-compatible sites to retain GIFs.

Lack of Native Creation and Authoring Tools

For an image format to thrive, end users and designers need accessible ways to create it. GIF was ubiquitous in virtually every image editor created since the late 1980s.

By contrast, animated WebP lacked native export capabilities in industry-standard software:

  • Adobe Photoshop and Adobe Premiere required third-party plugins for years before offering native WebP handling, and animated WebP exports remained cumbersome or non-existent in default menus.
  • Open-source tools like GIMP and ImageMagick were slow to implement full, user-friendly animated WebP workflows.
  • Casual creators relied on simple web converters rather than native software, creating friction that discouraged widespread production.

Competition from Looped Video Formats

During the exact window WebP needed to establish itself, major social networks and media hubs devised a different solution to the "GIF problem." Instead of switching to an animated image format, platforms like Imgur, Twitter (X), and Reddit began automatically converting uploaded GIFs into looped, muted MP4 or WebM video containers.

Video codecs (such as H.264) provided even better compression ratios than animated WebP and benefited from ubiquitous hardware acceleration across almost every mobile chip. Because platforms handled the conversion behind the scenes while still calling the media "GIFs," regular users and site operators felt little incentive to transition to WebP files directly.

CPU Overhead and Performance Concerns

Early implementations of animated WebP relied heavily on CPU-bound software decoding. While static WebP files decode quickly, animated WebP playback could cause noticeable CPU spikes and battery drain on low-power mobile devices. Modern platforms already had dedicated hardware decoders optimized for H.264 video, making muted video playback significantly less resource-intensive than decoding complex multi-frame WebP files in memory.

Cultural Inertia of the "GIF"

Beyond technical obstacles, GIF had evolved from an image format into an internet subculture. The word "GIF" became synonymous with short, looping animations and reaction media. Content delivery networks, search engines like Tenor and Giphy, and social media APIs were engineered entirely around the legacy format. Overcoming decades of accumulated infrastructure required an overwhelming technical mandate that animated WebP—caught between entrenched legacy GIFs and hyper-efficient modern video formats—could not easily provide.
